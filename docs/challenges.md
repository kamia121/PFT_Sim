# Learner challenges: what would help, what is built, what is not

Teaching simulator only. This document answers the project physician's request of 2026-10-21 to propose challenges that learners could benefit from. It rests on two reviews: a critical consultation (Opus 5.5, high reasoning) of the report-first plan, and a literature search whose evidence and its limits are in `docs/research/quality-and-interpretation-evidence.md`. A challenge that needs a threshold the project has not verified in a primary source is marked, and nothing here asks a learner whether to treat, whether a bronchodilator response diagnoses asthma, or what to do clinically at a given severity.

## The case for challenging the learner on quality

The evidence that matters most for training is that quality is the weakest link in routine spirometry and that readers disagree about it. Between 13% and 98% of routine traces are adequate depending on the setting and the criteria; in a series of 36 hospitals only half of the reports met end-of-test criteria; technologists labelled 54% of NHANES curves acceptable while 93% carried a usable FEV1; specialists disagreed with primary-care readers on test quality in 32% of tests (kappa 0.07). A learner who is shown only a finished report with a clean statement never practices the step that is most often wrong. No audit measures how often a technologist's quality statement is wrong, so the simulator's frequencies of misleading statements are teaching choices, and none is printed anywhere in the interface.

## Built in this round

The first challenge asks the learner to say which maneuvers are acceptable for the FEV1 and which for the FVC, separately, and then to grade the pre-bronchodilator and the post-bronchodilator sets (A to F and U, ATS/ERS 2019), because a cough spoils the FEV1 of one maneuver and not its FVC and because pre and post sets are graded separately. The grading rules were compared line by line with the 2019 statement, so this is the best-founded challenge in the set. The second asks whether the loop printed on page 1 is acceptable and, if not, which fault it shows; the key is the fault list of the displayed maneuver and never the shortfall from the tabulated FVC, because the tabulated FEV1 and FVC are maxima across acceptable maneuvers and a correct loop can fall short of them. The third applies the 2022 rule for the bronchodilator response (change as a percent of the predicted value) beside the printout's percent-of-baseline column and asks whether the post-bronchodilator set can be interpreted; the grade below which a post set is judged uninterpretable is a project choice and is marked as such, and the 10% boundary itself is verified only in an accepted manuscript. The fourth asks whether the technologist's statement agrees with the maneuvers (agrees, overstates, understates, or says nothing), staged so that the statement cannot answer the grade questions before they are answered. The fifth asks whether the DLCO test is acceptable by the simulator's criteria and whether the plethysmography FRC is acceptable and repeatable. The technologist's statement is generated with several kinds of error: software that grades only what it can detect and so misses a cough, a glottic closure, a leak or variable effort; a generic "acceptable and reproducible" line; an optimistic line over poor maneuvers; a pessimistic line over acceptable ones, as when a low peak flow is disease and not poor effort; carried-forward text that names something that was not done; and silence.

### Basic and advanced review (2026-10-21)

The maneuver-level items above are the advanced review. By default the practice page asks one question about the whole study (interpretable, interpretable with limitations, insufficient to interpret), because asking a trainee to mark every maneuver teaches transcription of a report that the technologist has already graded and hides the clinical question, which is what the findings can support. The Quality challenge control (off, on, varies) is a disclosure setting and is independent of the level; with the challenge off and the advanced level the grades are printed on page 2, so those items test how the report is read. The rule, its sources and the consultation are in SPEC.md ("Quality review level").

## Next, in order of value for cost

Two challenges teach a point that the evidence supports strongly and cost little. The first contrasts the fixed cut-offs (FEV1/FVC below 0.70, FEV1 below 80% of predicted) with the lower limit of normal across ages, and asks at which age the two classifications part; in a series of 40,646 adults the fixed ratio mislabelled up to 17% to 45% of healthy older men as obstructed, and in another 7.5% of tests disagreed, 16% over age 74. It is keyed by direct computation and needs no unverified threshold, and it should be presented as two conventions that disagree, never as an acceptable rule. The second shows the same values under GLI-2012, GLI Global and, later, NHANES III and asks which classification flips and whether a result is borderline; equation switching changed the category of 3% to 10% of tests in large series, and the simulator already computes the flips. The meaning of "borderline" (the project's 0.25 z band) is a teaching convention that no source defines, and the challenge says so.

A third group sharpens physiology rather than procedure. The DLCO challenge asks the learner to read VI/VC, the breath-hold time, the hemoglobin and the carboxyhemoglobin before reading the result, and to say which way a missing hemoglobin moves the interpretation; the constants come from a secondary source and an unread primary text, the magnitude of the carboxyhemoglobin effect conflicts between sources, and the simulator's DLCO classes are not the letters of the 2017 table, so the challenge asks for acceptable or not and not for a letter. A challenge that reads DLCO, VA and KCO together separates parenchymal loss from incomplete inflation, with the numeric KCO bands unverified beyond an abstract. A challenge on height and reference sex asks which way the z-scores move when the true height replaces the stated one (arm span misclassified 14% to 24% of subjects in one series); the simulator already stores both heights, the wording on sex stays neutral, and no source describes age or sex entry errors, so detection heuristics for those would be invention. A free-text impression challenge has the learner write a short statement and then compare it with the composed statement through a checklist (quality qualification, pattern, severity, bronchodilator response, DLCO, caveats, equation) that the learner applies to their own text; it is not scored automatically, because keyword matching would reintroduce the lexical cues that the quiz already removed, and the composed text is presented as one acceptable version, not the only wording. A caveat-selection challenge with decoys and the direction of error (an early termination lowers the FVC and raises the ratio, so obstruction is understated) reuses the existing caveat catalog.

## Later, and why

A prior-visit table, as on the printout, would let a learner decide whether a change lies within measurement variability and would allow a deliberate change of reference equation between visits (absolute FEV1 unchanged, percent of predicted moved). It needs a prior-visit record from its own random stream and a threshold for real change that the project has not verified: the 2005 percentages are known only from a secondary source, the 2022 standard's rule is described by secondary sources as built on FEV1Q with no DLCO or FVC thresholds, the DLCO intersession figures come from one abstract, and the ARTP statement advises showing serial absolute values and warns that cross-sectional z-scores are unsuitable for monitoring. Until the primary text is read the table could show absolute values and the equation effect and leave "real change" unscored; the challenge must never use the word progression. The plethysmography challenge on cross-checks (VA against the plethysmographic TLC, slow VC against FVC) and a hands-on choice of panting frequency wait for a rule set that is not secondary: the 5% tolerance and the lower FRC grades come from a secondary table, and the 2023 grading system could not be read.

## Deferred or rejected, with reasons

Including a rejected plethysmography trial or a cough maneuver in a reported value would be the most realistic laboratory error, but the simulator never does it and doing it would make the reported value differ from the correct one; it needs a second key computed from the trial table and is deferred. A "report or repeat" decision with one simulated DLCO test would always be "repeat", because the 2017 standard requires two acceptable tests; it waits for replicate tests. Unit traps (cmH2O against kPa, height in inches, hemoglobin in g/L) are low in value and DLCO in SI units is not implemented. The slow vital capacity section of the ATS 2017 sample report is not built because the simulator has no slow vital capacity maneuver. Challenges that ask whether to treat, what a bronchodilator response implies about asthma, or what to do at a given severity are rejected, because the project makes no patient-treatment recommendation.

## What the evidence does not support

There is no study of deliberate practice with feedback, error-detection exercises or pattern-recognition curricula for pulmonary function testing, and the educational studies found are small, abstract-only, and conflicting (a two-hour taught session raised a 25-student cohort's skills from 18% to 58% against 22% for self-study; neither lecture nor case-method continuing education improved general practitioners' interpretation in another). The claim that these challenges improve interpretation is therefore a design hypothesis, and the practice page records answers only in the browser, so the project cannot yet test it.
